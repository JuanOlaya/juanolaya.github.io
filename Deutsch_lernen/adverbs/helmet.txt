\section{User Study}

The user study evaluated how effectively the \textit{TurnHelmet} communicates directional information through weight-shifting and whether this mechanism can encourage head movement consistent with shoulder checking. The study focused on three distinct movement patterns—\textit{rotation}, \textit{step}, and \textit{swing}—and assessed their effect on accuracy, response time, confidence, and perceived workload.

\subsection{Study Design}

A within-subject, counterbalanced design was employed to minimize learning and order effects. Each participant experienced all three movement patterns, with the sequence of conditions determined using a Latin Square design (3! = 6 possible orders). This ensured that every pattern appeared equally often in each position across participants.

In total, \textbf{24 participants} (17 men, 6 women, 1 non-binary; aged 20–33 years, $M = 26.4$, $SD = 2.8$) took part in the study. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no neck or vestibular issues. All were right-handed. Each session lasted approximately 30 minutes, including briefing, training, and data collection.

Each participant completed three conditions corresponding to the three haptic movement patterns. For each condition, participants performed ten randomized trials—five with clockwise (right) rotation and five with counterclockwise (left) rotation.

\subsection{Measurements}

To evaluate both objective performance and subjective perception, the following metrics were collected:

Objective and subjective data were collected to evaluate both performance and perception. Accuracy was measured as the proportion of correctly identified movement directions, and response time was defined as the duration between the onset of motion and the participant’s response. After each trial, participants rated their confidence in the perceived direction on a seven-point Likert scale. To assess workload, mental and physical demand were recorded using the respective subscales of the NASA TLX questionnaire after each condition.

In addition, after each condition, participants answered two open-ended questions: what helped them to perceive the direction and what hindered them from perceiving it. At the end of the study, they were invited to share improvement suggestions and general feedback on the experience.


\subsection{Tasks and Procedure}

At the beginning of each session, participants read and signed an informed consent form. They were introduced to the \textit{TurnHelmet}, its purpose, and safety precautions. Before the main trials, participants completed a short training phase in which they experienced each of the three haptic patterns once. During this familiarization, participants received feedback on whether their directional judgments were correct to ensure task comprehension.

In the main study, each condition followed the same procedure. Participants sat upright in front of a computer while wearing the \textit{TurnHelmet}. They placed their hands on a keyboard and were instructed to identify the perceived rotation direction by pressing the left or right arrow key. Each trial began with the experimenter triggering the movement; the rotation continued until the participant responded. Immediately afterward, participants rated their confidence level.

After each block of ten trials, participants completed the NASA TLX subscales (mental and physical demand) and answered the two qualitative questions. Short breaks were offered between conditions to prevent fatigue or discomfort.

This controlled laboratory setup allowed for consistent measurement of how effectively the three weight-shifting patterns supported directional perception and head movement.
